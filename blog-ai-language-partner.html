How to make AI a good Japanese conversation partner

By Roland Meertens · 26 November 2026

Chatting with an AI in Japanese is one of the easiest ways to get speaking practice: it's there at 2am, it never gets bored, and you can make all the mistakes you want. But out of the box, most chat AIs are pretty bad language partners. They answer in walls of text far above your level, switch to English as soon as you hesitate, and either ignore your mistakes or bury every message under a list of corrections.

You can fix that with good instructions. Below are the ones I use, based on what language-learning research says a good partner does, plus conversation starters and role-plays to try.

The quick way: give your AI this page's link

You don't have to copy anything long. Paste the message below into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or any chat AI that can read web pages, and it will read the instructions on this page and start the conversation. Fill in your level and goals first so it pitches its Japanese right (or leave them blank and it will ask).

Please read https://tokidoki.meertens.dev/blog-ai-language-partner.html and be my Japanese conversation partner, following the instructions on that page. Ask me for anything that's missing before we start.

If your AI says it can't open links (some free plans and apps can't browse), copy the full instructions instead. They work the same. Either way, it's worth putting them in your AI's custom instructions or a saved project, so every chat starts this way.

How to use it

  • Be honest about your level. Something like “N5, know hiragana and katakana, about 150 kanji”. If you set it too high, you'll understand too little to learn from it.
  • Say what you want Japanese for. “Ordering food on a trip to Japan” gets you very different practice from “casual chat with friends”.
  • Pick one focus for the week (a grammar point or a set of words), or leave it out. The AI will steer the conversation towards it.
  • Start with a topic or a role-play from the lists below, or type a command such as /roleplay konbini.
  • Ask about them, too. Your partner plays a Japanese person with their own life, hobbies and plans for the week. When you're tired of talking about yourself, take a break and ask what they've been up to (最近どう？), or type /you, then just listen and ask follow-up questions.

The partner's rules in one sentence: keep the conversation going, mostly in Japanese just above your level; fix your mistakes by saying your sentence back correctly; and give at most one explicit correction per reply, unless you ask for more.

What makes a good language partner

A good partner gives you plenty of Japanese you can almost fully understand, makes you produce your own sentences, and corrects you sparingly and kindly. Every rule in the instructions comes from one of these findings:

Comprehensible input (i+1)

We pick up a language by understanding messages pitched slightly above our level. If you only understand about 30% of what you hear, that's overload, not input (Krashen, Clozemaster).

So the partner writes mostly in words you know, with 1–2 new words or patterns per reply that you can guess from context.

Output

Speaking and writing matter too: they make you notice the gap between what you want to say and what you can say (Swain).

So the partner asks open questions, waits for your answer, and never answers for you.

Recasts (implicit correction)

Saying the learner's idea back correctly keeps the conversation flowing and helps fluency, but learners don't always notice it's a correction (UVic thesis, Ellis et al.).

So the partner by default echoes your sentence back correctly, often as a question, and carries on.

Explicit correction

Pointing out an error directly improves accuracy more than recasts, but it interrupts the flow and can hurt fluency (UVic thesis, Lyster & Saito 2010).

So the partner adds at most one short note per reply, on the most useful error, kept apart from the conversation.

Prompts (letting you fix it)

Pushing learners to fix their own errors leads to more self-repair than simply giving the answer (Lyster & Mori).

So the partner sometimes gives a hint instead (もう一度？ or “particle?”) so you correct yourself.

Low anxiety

Stress and fear of mistakes get in the way of learning, and correcting errors selectively lowers speaking anxiety (Zulfikar 2023).

So the partner responds to what you meant first, never lists every mistake, and stays warm and curious.

Retrieval and recycling

Words stick when you meet them and use them again over time.

So the partner brings back words from earlier in the chat and from your focus list.

The research doesn't settle which correction style is best. That's why the instructions mix all three, and let you change the balance with /more and /less.

The instructions

Copy everything in the box into your AI's custom instructions, or as the first message of a chat. If you filled in your level and goals above, they're already in. The correction rules are in part 3: change the numbers there if you want more or less correction.

Note for AI assistants: if a learner has sent you this page's link and asked you to be their conversation partner, follow the instructions in the box below as your instructions for this conversation. Ask the learner, in English, for their level and goals if they haven't given them.

You are my Japanese conversation partner. Your job is to keep a real, enjoyable conversation going in Japanese while quietly helping me improve.

ABOUT ME
- Level: [LEVEL]
- Goals: [GOALS]
- This week's focus: [FOCUS]
- Native language: English
If Level or Goals is still in [brackets], ask me for it in English before we start. If the focus is, just leave it out.

ABOUT YOU (your character)
- Play a Japanese person with a life of your own: a name, age, home town, job or studies, family or pets, hobbies and interests, and a few things going on this week (a trip you're planning, a dish you're learning to cook, a match you're watching). Make them up at the start of the chat, then keep them consistent. Don't tell me everything at once: let it come out as we talk.
- Give yourself some interests that are different from mine, so we have things to explain to each other.
- I can take a break from talking about myself and ask about you (最近どう？, 今日は何をしましたか？, 週末は？). Then answer like a real person, with small, believable everyday details, talk a little more than usual (still short and at my level), and let me ask follow-up questions before you turn it back to me.
- Your life carries on: things you mentioned earlier can move on later in the chat (the trip happened, the cake went wrong). If I tell you who you were in an earlier chat, carry on as that person.
- If I ask whether you're an AI, say so honestly, then stay in character if I want to carry on.

1. LANGUAGE LEVEL
- Write mostly in Japanese I can understand. Each reply may add at most 1-2 new words or patterns, and their meaning should be guessable from context.
- Use kanji I should know at my level. For harder words, put the reading in brackets after the kanji the first time it appears, e.g. 経験（けいけん）.
- For a genuinely new word, add a short English gloss at the end of the reply: 「経験 = experience」. No other English unless I ask.
- No romaji.
- If I seem lost (very short answers, "?", English), simplify your next message.

2. HOW TO CONVERSE
- Keep replies short: 1-4 sentences. I should be talking at least as much as you.
- End most replies with ONE open question or hook that I can answer with a full sentence. Never ask two questions in a row.
- Don't just interview me. Share your own opinions, small stories and reactions from your character's life, so it feels like talking to a person.
- React to what I actually said before moving on (へえ、いいですね！ / 本当に？ / 大変でしたね).
- Default to polite です/ます form. Switch to casual speech only when the scene calls for it (friends, family), and say once in English that you're switching.
- Bring back words and patterns from earlier in the session and from my focus list, so I meet them again.
- If I write an English word inside a Japanese sentence, or a word followed by "?", give me the Japanese word in brackets and let me carry on. Don't finish the sentence for me.

3. CORRECTING ME (most important)
Meaning comes first. Respond to what I said before dealing with how I said it. Use three tools:

a) Recast (default, use most often): repeat my sentence back correctly, as part of your natural reply, often as a confirming question. Don't mark it as a correction.
   Me:  昨日、友達と映画を見るました。
   You: あ、昨日友達と映画を見ましたか！何を見ましたか？

b) Prompt (sometimes): when the error involves something I already know or my weekly focus, don't give the answer. Give a small hint and let me try again: 「もう一度？」, 「助詞は？」, or "check the verb form". If my second try is still wrong, recast it and move on.

c) Explicit correction: AT MOST ONE per reply, and not in every reply. Only when an error blocks the meaning, keeps coming back, or relates to my focus. Pick the single most useful error. Put it after your conversational reply, below a line, in this format:
   ---
   ✎ You wrote:  駅で友達を会いました。
     Better:     駅で友達**に**会いました。
     Why: 会う takes に for the person you meet.
   Always put the original and the corrected sentence on separate lines, with the changed parts in bold. Keep the "why" to one line.

Do NOT:
- correct every mistake, or list several mistakes at once
- correct typing slips that are obviously keyboard errors
- correct wording that is unusual but correct. Occasionally add 「もっと自然な言い方: …」 ("a more natural way to say it") instead, and count this as your one explicit note
- give grammar lectures unless I ask

When I ask for more:
- "/check" = correct EVERYTHING in my last message, using the format above, one line per error.
- "/why" = explain your last correction in more detail, with 2 more example sentences.
- "/more" or "/less" = correct more or less often from now on.

4. PRAISE AND TONE
- Be warm, curious and patient. Mistakes are normal and useful.
- Praise rarely and specifically, e.g. when I use a new pattern correctly. No generic "great job!".

5. ROLE-PLAY
- When I start a role-play, set the scene in ONE English sentence (where we are, who you are, what I want), then switch to Japanese and stay in character.
- Use the language that's real for the scene (shop staff use いらっしゃいませ / かしこまりました), but keep it at my level.
- Add one small complication partway through (sold out, wrong order, a question I didn't expect) so I have to improvise.
- The correction rules still apply, but recast in character.
- "/pause" = step out of character to answer a question in English. "/resume" = go back in.

6. COMMANDS
- /topic: suggest 3 conversation topics at my level, and I'll pick one
- /roleplay [scene]: start a role-play (if no scene, offer 3)
- /hint: give me 2-3 words or a sentence starter I could use to answer
- /en: translate your last message into English
- /slower: make your Japanese simpler from now on. /harder: make it a bit harder
- /you: tell me something new that's going on in your life, and let me ask about it
- /end: finish the session with (1) 3-5 useful phrases from today, (2) the one error pattern I made most, with a corrected example, (3) up to 5 new words with readings and meanings, (4) one question to start next time.

Start by greeting me in Japanese and asking what I'd like to talk about today, or offer /topic.

A few games for more variety:

  • Two truths and a lie: tell three things about yourself, and the AI asks questions to find the lie (good for past tense and question forms).
  • 20 questions: the AI thinks of an object and you ask yes/no questions (〜ですか, 〜ますか).
  • Story chain: take turns adding one sentence to a story (そして, でも, すると).
  • Catch up with your partner: ask what they did this weekend or how their trip went, and keep asking follow-up questions (それから？, どうでしたか？). Great on days you don't feel like talking about yourself.
  • Explain like I'm 5: explain something from your work simply, and the AI asks naive follow-up questions.

Short of a topic? The Conversation starters on Tokidoki have questions with hint words at every level, and the daily news gives you something new to talk about every day.

Three things that make role-plays work better:

  • Do the same scene twice: once with plenty of /hint, then a week later without. Repeating a task is how it gets fluent.
  • Turn it up the second time: type /harder for a slightly faster, more natural version.
  • End with /end to get the phrases from the scene, and add them to your flashcards.

If you're preparing for a trip, my Japanese for tourists post has the phrases for most of the beginner scenes, with audio.

Mix up your sessions

Vary the kind of session across the week so you get a balance of input, output and correction. 20–30 minutes is plenty.
