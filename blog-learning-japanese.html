How I'm learning Japanese: tips for every level

By Roland Meertens · 20 October 2026

The Japanese language is INSANELY hard to learn! Personally I have been learning Japanese for about a year, and tried many different methods. My level is still beginner (roughly JLPT N5). Still, it's fun to try to learn a completely new language, and Japanese people are very grateful for anyone trying to learn their language!

Here are my recommendations for different levels of proficiency.

Absolute beginner

Duolingo! It will teach you the basic words, how to order basic food, teaches some basic grammar principles, and makes a good start at the alphabet! Longer term I would not continue with Duolingo, as it feels like I'm doing more “pattern matching” and repeating old words rather than putting a big focus on generating text myself, and having a hard focus on words I don't know yet.

Japan has three alphabets:

  • Hiragana (ひらがな) — the “basic” alphabet for Japanese words
  • Katakana (カタカナ) — the alphabet for words imported from other countries
  • Kanji (漢字(かんじ)) — an alphabet with over 2000 characters which depict concepts or feelings

You should start with Hiragana, then do Katakana, and only when you need to, start learning the most used Kanji characters (if you learn the 100 most common ones you will recognise them on the street!). I can recommend the apps “Memory Hint Hiragana” and “Memory Hint Katakana” (free, from the Japan Foundation; also on Android). These apps give visual hints to remember the letters. The hints are sometimes quite bad/weird, which actually makes it easier to remember them.

Still, I was amazed and disappointed that in Japanese daily life these three alphabets get mixed together all the time in the same sentence. It means that by just living in Japan you can't really learn the language, until you learn all the words…

Motivated beginner

Once you decided that you enjoyed learning the alphabets and did some Duolingo, you can upgrade to try to speak a bit of Japanese and interact with people. For me two things helped here.

“Handy Japanese Conversations”. It's common phrases which you can commonly use — from how to order coffee and tea, to how to indicate where you are from! The book is fun, has phrases you can genuinely use (I put bookmarks at phrases I wanted to try in real life), and teaches a bit about Japanese culture!

Japanese people love it if you try to speak Japanese, and will quickly compliment you! Make sure to learn the different ways to say “your Japanese is great” (日本語(にほんご)が上手(じょうず)ですね), as you will hear it a lot in this phase. Make sure to be humble and reply with “not yet, not yet” — まだまだです (mada mada desu). Japanese people actually mean “wow, you look like a foreigner, and still sound like a foreigner who is making an attempt at learning!”. Once your Japanese improves you will hear it less and less! Only when your Japanese skills are amazing will your boss tell you that your Japanese sucks — make sure to aim for that level!

Immersion — videos

Japanese sounds very different from other languages. It's good to try to “immerse” in the culture by reading and listening a lot, already from the start. Unfortunately, because the language sounds so different, it's not super useful to immerse in normal content (e.g. Japanese movies for grown-ups). Content I would recommend which you can probably understand:

  • Comprehensible Japanese — a YouTube channel where someone only speaks Japanese, but very slowly. They point to the concept they are talking about. It helped my brain understand what some words mean. Sort their videos by most watched and only watch the “Complete beginner” videos.
  • Ken-san — a YouTube channel which is good once you know a few more words. He knows exactly which words are easy, and the words he uses are generally good to learn. Only watch the “N5” videos.
  • Totoro — a movie by Studio Ghibli. It's about children, and thus uses relatively easy language.

Unfortunately there is not a lot of fun native content for absolute beginners. Once you are a bit more advanced you can start watching Pokémon Concierge on Netflix, but the gap to understanding native content is IMO quite big.

Immersion — books

It's hard to find reading material in Japanese which is both easy and fun. However, there are so-called “graded readers” which focus on different levels of Japanese. The ones I found which I liked are:

  • Genki graded readers — fit well if you read the Genki textbook to learn grammar! There are two small extra stories for each chapter, so you generally already know the words and the grammar concepts they try to explain.
  • PAID Tadoku graded readers! Tadoku makes graded readers, but they are hard to buy outside of Japan. You can find them in larger Japanese bookshops though. The paid ones are of good quality, although sometimes the words are a bit all over the place. Still, it's enjoyable. Start with level “start”, then go to level zero, and then sloooowly work your way up.
  • FREE Tadoku graded readers! Tadoku also has free graded readers. It feels to me like these are made by enthusiasts rather than an official editorial team. Hence the content can go from way too boring (all these objects are blue) to way too difficult (what the heck is that Chihuahua doing…). Still, it's good to learn the words they use, sentences are short, and there are pictures to go with the text.
  • Manga — it took me a year to get to a point where I can start understanding and enjoying manga. I can recommend Yotsuba&! (よつばと!). Unfortunately, the language in the manga is quite casual, which means a lot of words are suddenly different 🫠. You can use this YouTube video along with it to get explanations. You can also get a reading pack so you have the translations of the words in an easy PDF.

Actually learning Japanese words + grammar!

Japanese has extensive grammar, and there are multiple resources to use for learning. I personally bought the Genki textbook. I think the book is great for learning, but it feels like it's better suited for a classroom setting than on your own. I worked through a few chapters with online teachers, which helped me a lot. I also sometimes go back and read a chapter about a grammar point or concept if I want to get better at it.

What makes the textbooks and lessons hard is having to learn both words AND the grammar concepts at the same time. I therefore recommend to also just die-hard learn a lot of individual words.

To memorise words I used Anki. It's a flashcard app which shows you words when you are just about to forget them. It does mean that it's good if you spend an hour every day refreshing your words, as otherwise your brain gets overwhelmed… A good deck to learn is this one, with all the words and concepts in the Genki textbooks. It helps me a lot if I can focus either on words or on grammar, so very much recommended.

Learning to work in Japan

If you are living in Japan for a bit longer and want to get fluent in speaking with colleagues: NHK has got you covered with Easy Japanese for Work! I didn't watch this a lot, but it seems fun to learn concepts about Japanese work culture, see the quizzes they do with people who have been here a while, and see how other foreigners are learning Japanese.

Teachers

I used italki to try some teachers. They can be relatively cheap, and it's a good way to get guidance. Use this referral link for a discount. You can trial a few teachers and then stick with the one you like best. My teacher is Ayaka, who works fine and knows the Genki textbooks and that style of teaching well.

Talking in real life

If you want to chat to people in real life I recommend going to the English Only Cafe in Tokyo. It's a place where a lot of language learners gather, and you can either join a Japanese table or an English table. Everyone is learning languages and is thus supportive of your journey. Every 30 minutes they ring a bell and you have to switch table and conversational partners, so you can practise a lot of concepts with a lot of different people.

I didn't try these events, but there are weekly free language exchanges where you speak English for half an hour with someone learning that language, and Japanese with that person. It's online so you can do it from anywhere.

Conclusion

Welcome to the hardest hobby you can think of! It's a longer journey than I expected, but it's rewarding once you are able to have small chats with your colleagues. Reach out to Roland if you are serious about language learning, as he always wants more learning partners (although he is only barely at an N5 level).
