How to learn Japanese with graded stories

By Roland Meertens · 26 October 2026

Textbooks teach you a grammar point, give you five example sentences, and move on. That's not enough to make it stick. What made the biggest difference for me was reading a lot of Japanese that I could actually understand. The trouble is that real Japanese (novels, newspapers, even most manga) is far too hard for a beginner. That's where graded stories come in.

What is a graded story?

A graded story (or “graded reader”) is written for learners on purpose: it only uses the words, kanji and grammar of a certain level. For Japanese the levels usually follow the JLPT, from N5 (beginner) up to N1. A good N5 story uses short です / ます sentences about everyday things, puts furigana on every kanji, and often has pictures that help you guess the words you don't know.

Because you understand most of what you read, you read fast, you read more, and you see the same words and grammar again and again in new sentences. That repetition is what moves a word from “I studied this once” to “I just know this”.

How to read them

The Japanese “tadoku” (多読(たどく), “reading a lot”) method has four simple rules that work well:

  • Start easier than you think. If you need to look up a word in every sentence, the story is too hard. Drop a level. You should understand almost everything on the page.
  • Don't reach for the dictionary. Try to guess from the context and the pictures first. Most words come back a few pages later, and by then you'll often know what they mean.
  • Skip what you don't get. One confusing sentence doesn't matter. Keep going.
  • Quit boring stories. Pick another one. Reading should be fun, otherwise you won't do enough of it.

A few things I'd add:

  • Listen while you read. Many graded stories come with audio. Reading along with a native voice teaches you the rhythm and pitch of the sentences, and helps your listening at the same time.
  • Re-read. The second time through a story is faster and more fun, and it's where the words really stick.
  • Pick out a few words, not all of them. After a story, save the two or three words that kept coming back into your flashcards (e.g. Anki), ideally together with the sentence you found them in.
  • Move up slowly. When a level starts to feel easy, read one or two stories at the next level. If they're a struggle, go back for a while. There's no rush.

Graded readers (books)

  • Tadoku free graded readers — free stories from level “start” upwards, with pictures and short sentences. The quality varies (some are very simple, some suddenly much harder), but they're a great free starting point.
  • Tadoku paid graded readers — the printed series, in levels from beginner up, with audio. Nicely made and well graded. They're easiest to find in larger bookshops in Japan.
  • Genki readers — if you study with the Genki textbook, the short stories that go with each chapter only use words and grammar you have already seen.

Easy news

  • NHK News Web Easy — real NHK news rewritten in easy Japanese, with furigana and audio. It's written for people living in Japan rather than for absolute beginners, so it fits better around N4–N3.
  • Todaii Easy Japanese (app) — easy news articles, many from NHK News Web Easy, with furigana you can switch off, a tap-to-look-up dictionary, and every word coloured by its JLPT level, so you can see at a glance whether an article fits you.

Graded listening

Stories don't have to be read. Nihongo con Teppei has a beginner podcast with short episodes of a few minutes, in clear, simple Japanese. The Comprehensible Japanese YouTube channel tells simple stories while pointing at pictures. Both are great for a commute.

Graded stories on Tokidoki

Of course I built graded reading into this site too. Everything is free and works without an account:

  • Stories — 59 short stories from N5 to N3. Tap any word for its reading and meaning (and save it as a flashcard with its sentence), tap a sentence for the English and every grammar point it uses. Furigana and translations can be turned off once you don't need them anymore.
  • News — three real stories from Japan every day, each written at N5, N4 and N3. Read one at your level, then switch up a level to see the same story told with harder grammar. Every story has a recording, which you can also play slowed down.
  • Bunkei in the Wild — six stories that each run from N5 to N3 and use every sentence pattern from the drills; after each sentence you pick what its pattern means.
  • Kuro the Word Detective — a detective story where you move the case on by answering in Japanese. Ten cases from N5 to N3.
  • Story Fill-in and Particle Stories — read a passage and fill in the missing words or particles, with an explanation for every blank.
  • Text Analysis — when you are ready for real texts, paste one in and get it split into sentences with furigana, meanings and grammar (needs your own Claude API key).

A simple routine

If you want something to start with tomorrow: read one story a day at a level where you understand almost everything, listen to it once, save two or three new words, and re-read yesterday's story before you start today's. Fifteen minutes a day of easy reading beats one heroic hour with a dictionary on Sunday.
