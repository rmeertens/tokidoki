Japanese for tourists: the phrases you'll actually use

By Roland Meertens · 27 October 2026

Most phrasebooks give you two hundred sentences, and then on your first day in Japan the cashier at the convenience store asks you something that isn't in the book. As a tourist you don't need much Japanese, but you need the right Japanese: a handful of phrases you'll say twenty times a day, and, just as important, the phrases people will say to you.

This post is that short list, sorted by where you'll use it. Each phrase has the Japanese, the romaji to read it aloud, and what it really means in practice.

The five words that do most of the work

If you learn nothing else, learn these. With them, some pointing and a smile, you can get through most of a trip.

  • すみません sumimasen — “excuse me”, “sorry” and “thank you (for the trouble)” in one. Use it to call a waiter, to get past someone on the train, to stop a stranger and ask the way, and when someone holds a door for you.
  • ありがとうございます arigatō gozaimasu — “thank you”, the polite way. Plain ありがとう is for friends; with shop staff and strangers, add ございます.
  • お願(ねが)いします onegai shimasu — “please”. Put it after almost anything you want: これ、お願(ねが)いします (this one, please), 水(みず)、お願(ねが)いします (water, please). It also means “yes, please” when you're offered something.
  • 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です daijōbu desu — literally “it's fine”, but in shops it's the polite way to say “no, thank you”. Asked if you want a bag, a receipt or a point card? 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です, with a small wave of the hand.
  • これ kore — “this (one)”. Point at the menu picture, the snack in the display case or the ticket on the screen, and say これ. Add ください (kudasai, “please give me”) or お願(ねが)いします and you've ordered.

At the convenience store and in shops

The konbini is where you'll hear the most Japanese, and it's always the same few questions. You don't have to understand every word: listen for the key word and answer with お願(ねが)いします (yes, please) or 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です (no, thanks).

  • 袋(ふくろ)はご利用(りよう)ですか? fukuro wa go-riyō desu ka? — “Do you need a bag?” Plastic bags cost a few yen, so it's always asked. Listen for 袋(ふくろ).
  • 温(あたた)めますか? atatamemasu ka? — “Shall I heat it up?” for bento, onigiri and the like.
  • お箸(はし)はおつけしますか? ohashi wa otsuke shimasu ka? — “Would you like chopsticks?” (or スプーン, a spoon).
  • ポイントカードはお持(も)ちですか? pointo kādo wa omochi desu ka? — “Do you have a point card?” Answer 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です or ないです (nai desu, “I don't”).
  • 〜はありますか? … wa arimasu ka? — “Do you have …?” Hold up your phone with a photo and say これはありますか?
  • いくらですか? ikura desu ka? — “How much is it?” Prices are almost always shown, so you'll mostly need this at markets.
  • カードで払(はら)えますか? kādo de haraemasu ka? — “Can I pay by card?” Cards and IC cards (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA) work in most places now, but small restaurants and shrines are often cash only.
  • 試着(しちゃく)してもいいですか? shichaku shite mo ii desu ka? — “May I try this on?”
  • 免税(めんぜい)できますか? menzei dekimasu ka? — “Can I buy this tax-free?” Look for the 免税 / Tax-Free sign, and have your passport with you.

Paying: put your money in the small tray on the counter rather than handing it over. And don't tip — anywhere. It's not expected, and a tip left on the table may well be chased after and returned to you.

In restaurants

  • 何名様(なんめいさま)ですか? nanmei-sama desu ka? — “How many people?” the moment you walk in. Answer with fingers and 一人(ひとり)です (hitori desu, one), 二人(ふたり)です (futari, two), 三人(さんにん)です (sannin, three).
  • 英語(えいご)のメニューはありますか? eigo no menyū wa arimasu ka? — “Do you have an English menu?”
  • すみません! — to call the staff over. It's normal to say it out loud, even across the room; waiting to catch someone's eye can take all evening. Many places also have a call button on the table.
  • おすすめは何(なん)ですか? osusume wa nan desu ka? — “What do you recommend?” A great question at small places with no pictures.
  • これを一(ひと)つください kore o hitotsu kudasai — “One of these, please.” 二(ふた)つ (futatsu) is two, 三(みっ)つ (mittsu) three.
  • 生(なま)ビール nama bīru — draught beer, often shortened to 生(なま): 生(なま)二(ふた)つ! Water and tea are usually free: お水(みず)ください.
  • 〜が食(た)べられません … ga taberaremasen — “I can't eat …”. Fill in 肉(にく) (meat), 魚(さかな) (fish), 卵(たまご) (egg) or えび (shrimp). For a serious allergy, carry it written down in Japanese as well: dashi (fish stock) is in almost everything.
  • いただきます itadakimasu before you eat, and ごちそうさまでした gochisōsama deshita when you leave. Saying the second one to the chef on the way out always gets a smile.
  • お会計(かいけい)お願(ねが)いします okaikei onegai shimasu — “The bill, please.” Usually you take the bill to the register by the door rather than paying at the table. 別々(べつべつ)で (betsubetsu de) asks to pay separately.

Many ramen and set-meal places work with a ticket machine (券売機(けんばいき)) by the door: pay, press the button with the picture you want, and hand the ticket to the staff.

Trains and finding your way

  • 〜はどこですか? … wa doko desu ka? — “Where is …?” The single most useful pattern: トイレはどこですか? (toilet), 駅(えき)はどこですか? (station), 出口(でぐち)はどこですか? (exit).
  • 〜に行(い)きたいです … ni ikitai desu — “I want to go to …”. Show the place on your phone at the same time.
  • この電車(でんしゃ)は〜に止(と)まりますか? kono densha wa … ni tomarimasu ka? — “Does this train stop at …?” Express and rapid trains skip stations, so this one is worth asking on the platform.
  • 右(みぎ) / 左(ひだり) / まっすぐ migi / hidari / massugu — right / left / straight on. Enough to follow most directions, especially with the pointing that comes with them.
  • 近(ちか)いですか? chikai desu ka? — “Is it close?” and 歩(ある)いて行(い)けますか? aruite ikemasu ka? — “Can I walk there?”

On the train you'll hear 次(つぎ)は〜 (tsugi wa …, “the next stop is …”) and ドアが閉(し)まります (doa ga shimarimasu, “the doors are closing”). Station names are almost always shown in English too, so the kanji you most want to recognise are 出口(でぐち) (exit), 入口(いりぐち) (entrance), 男(おとこ) and 女(おんな) (men and women, on toilets and onsen curtains).

At the hotel or ryokan

  • 予約(よやく)しています。〜です yoyaku shite imasu. … desu — “I have a reservation. My name is …”.
  • 荷物(にもつ)を預(あず)かってもらえますか? nimotsu o azukatte moraemasu ka? — “Could you keep my luggage?” before check-in or after check-out. Almost every hotel will.
  • Wi-Fiのパスワードは何(なん)ですか? waifai no pasuwādo wa nan desu ka? — “What's the Wi-Fi password?”
  • 朝(あさ)ごはんは何時(なんじ)からですか? asagohan wa nanji kara desu ka? — “What time is breakfast from?”

What you'll hear (and don't need to answer)

A lot of Japanese aimed at you is ritual politeness from shop and restaurant staff, in formal keigo. Knowing it saves a lot of stress, because most of it needs no answer at all.

  • いらっしゃいませ irasshaimase — “welcome”, called out when you walk in. Customers don't reply; a nod is plenty.
  • 少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちください shōshō omachi kudasai — “one moment, please”.
  • かしこまりました kashikomarimashita — “certainly”, after you've ordered or asked for something.
  • こちらへどうぞ kochira e dōzo — “this way, please”, as you're shown to a table.
  • 以上(いじょう)でよろしいですか? ijō de yoroshii desu ka? — “Will that be all?” Answer はい.
  • ありがとうございました arigatō gozaimashita — “thank you (for coming)”, as you leave. A small nod or your own ありがとうございます is a nice touch.

When you don't understand

These keep a conversation going instead of ending it, and people are usually very happy to help someone who tries.

  • もう一度(いちど)お願(ねが)いします mō ichido onegai shimasu — “Once more, please.”
  • ゆっくりお願(ねが)いします yukkuri onegai shimasu — “Slowly, please.”
  • 日本語(にほんご)が少(すこ)しだけわかります nihongo ga sukoshi dake wakarimasu — “I understand only a little Japanese.”
  • 英語(えいご)は大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですか? eigo wa daijōbu desu ka? — “Is English OK?” Softer than asking “do you speak English?”, which can put people on the spot.
  • わかりました wakarimashita — “Got it.” And わかりません wakarimasen — “I don't understand.”

If something goes wrong

  • 助(たす)けて! tasukete! — “Help!”
  • 救急車(きゅうきゅうしゃ)を呼(よ)んでください kyūkyūsha o yonde kudasai — “Please call an ambulance.”
  • 〜が痛(いた)いです … ga itai desu — “My … hurts”: 頭(あたま) (head), お腹(なか) (stomach).
  • 財布(さいふ)をなくしました saifu o nakushimashita — “I've lost my wallet.” Go to the nearest 交番(こうばん) (kōban, police box): lost things are handed in remarkably often in Japan.

Emergency numbers: 119 for an ambulance or fire, 110 for the police. The Japan National Tourism Organization runs a 24-hour Japan Visitor Hotline (050-3816-2787, or +81-50-3816-2787 from abroad) in English, Chinese and Korean.

A few tips that matter more than grammar

  • Add です and ます. As a guest, the polite form is always right. A short polite sentence beats a long casual one.
  • Lead with すみません. Before any question to a stranger, it signals “sorry to bother you” and gets you their attention politely.
  • Point and show. Pointing at a menu, a map or a photo on your phone is completely normal. One word plus お願(ねが)いします is a full sentence.
  • Learn katakana. A huge part of menus and signs is loanwords written in katakana: コーヒー, ホテル, チーズケーキ. Being able to sound them out turns half the menu into English. It takes a week or two.
  • Practise listening, not just speaking. Saying your phrase is the easy part; the trouble is the fast answer that comes back. Hearing the standard questions a few times before you go makes a big difference.

Practise before you go

Everything here comes up again on Tokidoki, free and without an account. The Phrases pages have each situation with readings, meanings and audio:

  • Everyday set phrases — すみません, いただきます and the rest of the daily basics.
  • In shops — what the cashier says, from 袋(ふくろ) to point cards.
  • Food and drinks — ordering, paying and eating out.
  • Stations and trains — the announcements you'll hear on the platform.
  • Asking the way and Ryokan and onsen.
  • Hospital and emergencies — the ones you hope you won't need.

Then learn to read menus with the katakana drills, and train your ear with the Listening questions, which are recorded with natural voices.

You'll never regret the effort: even a clumsy ありがとうございます changes how a conversation goes. いい旅(たび)を! — have a good trip!
