Japanese for tourists: the phrases you'll actually use

By Roland Meertens · 27 October 2026

Most phrasebooks give you two hundred sentences, and then on your first day the cashier at the convenience store asks you something that isn't in the book. As a tourist you don't need much Japanese, but you need the right Japanese: a handful of phrases you'll say twenty times a day, and the questions people will ask you, so you know what to answer.

You don't need to read Japanese for any of this. Every phrase is written out in romaji (Japanese in the Latin alphabet), with a recording you can play.

How this page works

  • The big line is what to say, in romaji. Under it is what it means, then the same phrase in Japanese script, for when you want to start learning to read it.
  • ▶ plays the phrase, 🐢 plays it slowly. Listen a few times and say it along with the voice.
  • Blue cards are things you say; orange cards are what you'll hear from staff, in a second voice. Under a question you'll be asked you'll find what to answer.

Reading the romaji

  • Vowels are always the same: a as in “father”, i as in “meet”, u as in “food” (short, lips relaxed), e as in “get”, o as in “more”.
  • A line over a vowel (ō, ū) means hold it twice as long. Kōban (police box) is “koh-ban”; it matters, because a short vowel can make another word.
  • A double consonant (mittsu, massugu) is a tiny pause, as in “cat tail”.
  • The u in desu and -masu is almost silent: they sound like “dess” and “mass”. Sumimasen often sounds like “s'mimasen”.
  • r is a light tap, somewhere between r, l and d. And give every syllable the same weight; Japanese has no strong stress.

The five words that do most of the work

If you learn nothing else, learn these. With them, some pointing and a smile, you can get through most of a trip.

You say

Sumimasen

Excuse me / sorry / thanks for the trouble

すみません

The most useful word in Japan. Use it to call a waiter, to get past someone on the train, to stop a stranger and ask the way, and when someone holds a door for you.

You say

Arigatō gozaimasu

Thank you

ありがとうございます

Plain arigatō is for friends. With staff and strangers, add gozaimasu.

You say

Onegai shimasu

Please / yes, please

お願(ねが)いします

Put it after anything you want (Mizu, onegai shimasu: water, please), or say it on its own to accept what you’re offered.

You say

Daijōbu desu

No, thank you (literally “I’m fine”)

大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です

The polite way to turn down a bag, a receipt or a point card. Add a small wave of the hand.

You say

Kore o kudasai

This one, please

これをください

Point at the menu picture, the snack in the display case or a photo on your phone. You’ve just ordered.

Counting to three

For ordering and for saying how many you are, these six cover almost every case. Show the number with your fingers too.

You say

Hitotsu

One (of something)

一(ひと)つ

Kore o hitotsu kudasai: one of these, please.

You say

Futatsu

Two

二(ふた)つ

You say

Mittsu

Three

三(みっ)つ

You say

Hitori desu

Just one person

一人(ひとり)です

People are counted differently from things.

You say

Futari desu

Two people

二人(ふたり)です

You say

San-nin desu

Three people

三人(さんにん)です

At the convenience store and in shops

The konbini is where you’ll hear the most Japanese, and it’s always the same few questions. You don’t need to catch every word: listen for the key word, then answer Onegai shimasu (yes, please) or Daijōbu desu (no, thanks).

You’ll hear

Irasshaimase!

Welcome!

いらっしゃいませ！

Called out when you walk in. Customers don’t answer it; a nod is plenty.

They ask

Fukuro wa go-riyō desu ka?

Do you need a bag?

袋(ふくろ)はご利用(りよう)ですか？

Bags cost a few yen, so you’ll always be asked. Listen for fukuro.

You answer

They ask

Atatamemasu ka?

Shall I heat it up?

温(あたた)めますか？

For bento, onigiri and the like.

You answer

They ask

Ohashi wa otsuke shimasu ka?

Would you like chopsticks?

お箸(はし)はおつけしますか？

You answer

They ask

Pointo kādo wa omochi desu ka?

Do you have a point card?

ポイントカードはお持(も)ちですか？

You answer

You say

Nai desu

I don’t (have one)

ないです

You say

Kore wa arimasu ka?

Do you have this?

これはありますか？

Say it while you show a photo on your phone.

You say

Ikura desu ka?

How much is it?

いくらですか？

You say

Kādo de haraemasu ka?

Can I pay by card?

カードで払(はら)えますか？

Cards and IC cards (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA) work in most places, but small restaurants and shrines are often cash only.

You say

Shichaku shite mo ii desu ka?

May I try this on?

試着(しちゃく)してもいいですか？

You say

Menzei dekimasu ka?

Can I buy this tax-free?

免税(めんぜい)できますか？

Look for the Tax-Free sign, and have your passport with you.

You’ll hear

Shōshō omachi kudasai

One moment, please

少々(しょうしょう)お待(ま)ちください

You’ll hear

Arigatō gozaimashita

Thank you (for coming)

ありがとうございました

As you leave. A nod, or your own arigatō gozaimasu, is a nice touch.

In restaurants

To call the staff, just say Sumimasen! out loud, even across the room; waiting to catch someone’s eye can take all evening. Many places also have a call button on the table.

They ask

Nanmei-sama desu ka?

How many people?

何名様(なんめいさま)ですか？

Asked the moment you walk in.

You answer

Hold up your fingers as you say it.

You’ll hear

Kochira e dōzo

This way, please

こちらへどうぞ

You say

Eigo no menyū wa arimasu ka?

Do you have an English menu?

英語(えいご)のメニューはありますか？

You say

Osusume wa nan desu ka?

What do you recommend?

おすすめは何(なん)ですか？

Great at small places with no pictures on the menu.

They ask

Go-chūmon wa okimari desu ka?

Are you ready to order?

ご注文(ちゅうもん)はお決(き)まりですか？

You answer

You say

Mō sukoshi matte kudasai

Could you give us a little longer?

もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってください

You say

Nama bīru, futatsu

Two draught beers

生(なま)ビール、二(ふた)つ

Often shortened to just nama.

You say

Omizu kudasai

Water, please

お水(みず)ください

Water and tea are usually free.

You say

Niku ga taberaremasen

I can’t eat meat

肉(にく)が食(た)べられません

Swap niku for sakana (fish), tamago (egg) or ebi (shrimp). For a serious allergy, carry it written down in Japanese too: fish stock (dashi) is in almost everything.

You’ll hear

Kashikomarimashita

Certainly

かしこまりました

After you’ve ordered.

They ask

Ijō de yoroshii desu ka?

Will that be all?

以上(いじょう)でよろしいですか？

You answer

You say

Hai

Yes

はい

You say

Itadakimasu

Said before you eat

いただきます

You say

Gochisōsama deshita

Thank you for the meal

ごちそうさまでした

Say it to the chef on your way out; it always gets a smile.

You say

Okaikei onegai shimasu

The bill, please

お会計(かいけい)お願(ねが)いします

Usually you take the bill to the register by the door instead of paying at the table. Don’t tip, anywhere: it isn’t expected.

You say

Betsubetsu de onegai shimasu

Separate bills, please

別々(べつべつ)でお願(ねが)いします

Trains and finding your way

The one pattern to know is … wa doko desu ka?, “where is …?”. Show the place on your phone at the same time, and the answer will come with a lot of pointing.

You say

Toire wa doko desu ka?

Where is the toilet?

トイレはどこですか？

You say

Eki wa doko desu ka?

Where is the station?

駅(えき)はどこですか？

You say

Shibuya ni ikitai desu

I want to go to Shibuya

渋谷(しぶや)に行(い)きたいです

Swap in any place name.

You say

Kono densha wa Shibuya ni tomarimasu ka?

Does this train stop at Shibuya?

この電車(でんしゃ)は渋谷(しぶや)に止(と)まりますか？

Express and rapid trains skip stations, so it’s worth asking on the platform.

You say

Aruite ikemasu ka?

Can I walk there?

歩(ある)いて行(い)けますか？

You’ll hear

Migi

Right

右(みぎ)

You’ll hear

Hidari

Left

左(ひだり)

You’ll hear

Massugu

Straight on

まっすぐ

You’ll hear

Tsugi wa, Shibuya desu

The next stop is Shibuya

次(つぎ)は、渋谷(しぶや)です

The station names are usually shown and announced in English too.

You’ll hear

Doa ga shimarimasu

The doors are closing

ドアが閉(し)まります

At the hotel or ryokan

You say

Chekku-in onegai shimasu

Check-in, please

チェックインお願(ねが)いします

You say

Yoyaku shite imasu

I have a reservation

予約(よやく)しています

They ask

Onamae o onegai shimasu

Your name, please

お名前(なまえ)をお願(ねが)いします

You answer

Just say your name, adding desu: “Smith desu.”

They ask

Pasupōto o onegai shimasu

Your passport, please

パスポートをお願(ねが)いします

Hotels must check foreign guests’ passports.

You answer

You say

Nimotsu o azukatte moraemasu ka?

Could you keep my luggage?

荷物(にもつ)を預(あず)かってもらえますか？

Before check-in or after check-out. Almost every hotel will.

You say

Waifai no pasuwādo wa nan desu ka?

What’s the Wi-Fi password?

ワイファイのパスワードは何(なん)ですか？

You say

Asagohan wa nanji kara desu ka?

What time is breakfast from?

朝(あさ)ごはんは何時(なんじ)からですか？

When you don’t understand

These keep a conversation going instead of ending it, and people are usually very happy to help someone who tries.

You say

Mō ichido onegai shimasu

Once more, please

もう一度(いちど)お願(ねが)いします

You say

Yukkuri onegai shimasu

Slowly, please

ゆっくりお願(ねが)いします

You say

Nihongo wa sukoshi dake desu

I only speak a little Japanese

日本語(にほんご)は少(すこ)しだけです

You say

Eigo wa daijōbu desu ka?

Is English OK?

英語(えいご)は大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですか？

Softer than “do you speak English?”, which can put people on the spot.

You say

Kaite moraemasu ka?

Could you write it down?

書(か)いてもらえますか？

Handy for prices and times.

You say

Wakarimasen

I don’t understand

わかりません

You say

Wakarimashita

Got it / I understand

わかりました

If something goes wrong

Emergency numbers: 119 for an ambulance or fire, 110 for the police. The Japan National Tourism Organization runs a 24-hour Japan Visitor Hotline (050-3816-2787, or +81-50-3816-2787 from abroad) in English, Chinese and Korean.

You say

Tasukete!

Help!

助(たす)けて！

You say

Kyūkyūsha o yonde kudasai

Please call an ambulance

救急車(きゅうきゅうしゃ)を呼(よ)んでください

You say

Byōin wa doko desu ka?

Where is a hospital?

病院(びょういん)はどこですか？

You say

Atama ga itai desu

I have a headache

頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです

Swap atama (head) for onaka (stomach) or point at what hurts.

You say

Saifu o nakushimashita

I’ve lost my wallet

財布(さいふ)をなくしました

Lost things are handed in remarkably often in Japan.

You say

Kōban wa doko desu ka?

Where is the police box?

交番(こうばん)はどこですか？

A kōban is a small neighbourhood police post, found near most stations.

A few tips that matter more than grammar

  • Stay polite. Words ending in desu and -masu are the polite form, and as a guest that's always right. A short polite sentence beats a long casual one.
  • Lead with sumimasen. Before any question to a stranger, it means “sorry to bother you” and gets you their attention politely.
  • Point and show. Pointing at a menu, a map or a photo on your phone is completely normal. One word plus onegai shimasu is a full sentence.
  • Pay with the tray, and don't tip. Put your money in the small tray on the counter rather than handing it over. Tipping isn't expected anywhere, and a tip left on the table may well be chased after and returned.
  • Listen more than you speak. Saying your phrase is the easy part; the trouble is the quick question that comes back. Play the orange cards a few times before you go, so you recognise them when you hear them.

Want to go a step further?

Learn katakana, one of the two Japanese alphabets. It's used for words borrowed from other languages, which is a big part of menus and signs: コーヒー is kōhī (coffee), ホテル is hoteru (hotel). Once you can sound it out, half the menu turns into English. It takes a week or two with the Kana drills.
